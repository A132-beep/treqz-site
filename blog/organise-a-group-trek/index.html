How to organise a group trek in India: a step-by-step plan

Plan a group trek in India: pick a trek for mixed fitness, choose an operator or go independent, then sort permits, roles, budget, safety and communication.

To organise a group trek, choose a trek for your slowest member, check the season and permits, pick an operator or an experienced leader, agree roles and budget, and write down a safety plan before anyone books a ticket. A group that agrees on these things beforehand has far fewer arguments on the trail.

Choosing a trek for a group

  • Plan for the weakest link. Choose the trek that suits your least experienced and least fit member, not the keenest.
  • Check days, altitude and difficulty on the trek's page in the 3D atlas. For example, Kedarkantha is listed at 3,810 m over five days, while Nagtibba is a three-day trek to 3,022 m and Triund is a two-day trek to 2,875 m.
  • Match the season. Each trek page lists best and acceptable months. Winter treks add snow and cold; monsoon treks add leeches and slippery trails.
  • Read the day-by-day table. Our guide to reading altitude gain shows how to spot the big days.
  • For first-timers, browse the easy treks guide and pick a short one.

As an example of what an operator listing says: one operator's Kedarkantha page lists a base price of ₹9,500 plus 5% GST per person. It states that it obtains the required permits for you, and includes tents, sleeping bags, a trek leader, a first-aid kit with an oxygen cylinder and an oximeter. It asks for ID copies and a medical certificate, and it does not include emergency evacuation or hospital costs. That page also gives two different guide-to-trekker ratios, so ask for the ratio directly. We name it only as a fact from its own page, not a recommendation.

Questions to ask any operator: What is included and excluded? Who is the leader and what training do they have? What is the group size and guide ratio? What happens if someone must turn back? Is evacuation covered?

Permits and paperwork

Many treks pass through forest or protected areas that need entry permits or fees, and some ask for ID. Read our trek permits guide and the permit section of each trek's page, and ask your operator who arranges them. Collect everyone's ID copies early, and carry the originals on the trek.

Fitness mix and acclimatisation

  • Ask each person honestly how they have trained. See getting fit for a Himalayan trek.
  • Set a pace for the group, not the fastest. A group that spreads out is harder to keep safe.
  • Know the altitude guideline. A summary of the Wilderness Medical Society's guidelines says unacclimatised people face higher risk above about 2,500 m, that sleeping altitude matters more than the highest point reached, and that sleeping altitude should rise by no more than about 500 m a night, with a rest day after every 1,000 m gained. Read altitude sickness on treks for the warning signs.
  • Medical conditions. Anyone with a health condition should speak to a doctor first, and tell the trek leader.

Roles in the group

  1. Group lead: final voice on route and timing, in agreement with the trek leader.
  2. Money keeper: collects and tracks the budget.
  3. Permits and ID person: gathers documents and keeps copies.
  4. Head-counter: counts the group at every stop.
  5. Sweeper: walks at the back with the slowest member.
  6. First-aid and health watch: checks in on how people feel and knows what to do if someone becomes unwell.
  7. Communications: posts updates to a home contact and keeps a power bank.

Decide in advance what happens if someone drops out: who gets a refund and who covers non-refundable costs. For typical costs see how much a trek costs.

A safety plan the whole group agrees

  • Turn-back rule: a time and conditions (weather, illness, snow) when the group turns back, decided before the walk.
  • Who decides: the leader, backed by the group.
  • Head-count and buddy system: never leave anyone behind alone.
  • First aid: know where the kit is and who can use it.
  • Heat and water: on lower or summer treks, drink regularly. The NHS lists dizziness, headache, nausea and heavy sweating as signs of heat exhaustion, and treats confusion with hot, dry skin as a medical emergency.
  • Emergency contacts: operator, local help numbers and a person at home who has your itinerary.

Communication

  • One group chat for planning, one document for the plan, and one person who updates it.
  • Plan for no signal. Download offline maps and the itinerary before you lose network. See offline maps for treks, and how Treqz works with no signal.
  • Tell people at home the trek, dates and expected return.
  • Decide how to handle disagreements on the trail: the leader decides, discussion afterwards.

Quick questions

How big should a group trek be?

Small enough that everyone can be heard and kept together. Operators set their own ratios: one Kedarkantha operator lists a guide-to-trekker ratio on its page, though it gives two different figures, so always ask directly.

Should we go with an operator or independently?

An operator is simpler for first-timers and often handles permits, tents, food and a first-aid kit. Going independently needs someone with real experience of that trail, plus permits, food, tents and a rescue plan you arrange yourselves.

How do we split costs fairly?

Agree a per-person budget before booking, collect it in one place, and keep a shared sheet. Decide in advance how to handle someone dropping out.
