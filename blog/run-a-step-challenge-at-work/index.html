How to run a step challenge at work: rules, scoring, privacy

A practical checklist for running a workplace step challenge in India: setup, rules, fair scoring, privacy, prizes and the mistakes that make people quit.

The 30-second answer
  • Decide the dates, teams, scoring and prizes before you announce it, and put the rules on one page.
  • Score by team average, count participation only and collect no health data.
  • Most challenges fail from unclear rules, uneven teams or too much pressure, not from lack of interest.

A good workplace step challenge needs four things: clear dates, fair teams, simple scoring and a promise that only participation is counted. Here is a checklist, the rules to write down, and the mistakes that make people drop out.

If you are still choosing a format, start with our 12 office fitness challenge ideas. This post assumes you have picked a step challenge.

Setup checklist

  1. Choose the dates. Three to four weeks. Avoid festival weeks, financial year-end and long leave periods.
  2. Pick an owner. One person (HR, admin or a volunteer) answers questions and posts updates.
  3. Form teams. Five to ten people each, mixing departments so friends do not all end up together. Let people choose their team if they wish.
  4. Choose how steps are counted. Phone health apps, watches or a manual weekly entry in a shared sheet all work. Allow more than one so no one needs to buy a device.
  5. Write the rules on one page (see below) and share it before the start day.
  6. Plan prizes and the finish. A small team lunch or an extra half-day is enough.
  7. Announce a rest and catch-up rule. People fall ill, travel and have family events; say how missed days are handled.

Rules worth writing down

  • What counts: steps from walking and running. Offer a minutes-based alternative for people who cannot use steps, such as swimming, cycling or wheelchair use.
  • Daily cap: set a sensible maximum per person per day and announce it. This prevents one extreme day from deciding everything.
  • How often to report: daily if automatic, weekly if manual.
  • Fair play: steps must come from real movement. Shaking a phone or lending a watch is out.
  • Opting out: anyone can leave at any time, without giving a reason.
  • Disputes: the organiser decides, and says so in advance.

Scoring that stays fair

Rank teams by average steps per person per day, not by total. A total rewards the biggest team. An average lets five people beat fifty.

Offer at least two prize categories (for example, top average and most improved) so effort is rewarded as well as fitness. As a yardstick, the World Health Organization suggests at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity a week for adults; you do not need to push people far beyond it for a challenge to be worthwhile.

Privacy: count participation, nothing else

Step counts linked to named people are personal data. Keep the data collected to the minimum:

  • Collect steps, minutes or days active. Do not collect weight, heart rate, sleep, location tracks or medical details.
  • Tell people what you collect, why, who sees it and when it will be deleted. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is built around consent that is free, specific and informed, a clear notice, and the ability to withdraw consent. Commentary from law firms describes these points; ask your legal team how it applies to your company.
  • Show team boards to everyone, and individual numbers only to the person themselves, unless people agree to share them.
  • Delete the data after the challenge, or keep only team totals.

Participation-only is also how Treqz plans Crew challenges: Crew is coming soon and is described on its page as participation only, with no private health data.

Prizes

  • Keep prizes small and shared: a team lunch, a trek day, a donation to a charity chosen by the winners.
  • Avoid large cash prizes. They encourage cheating and make non-winners feel left out.
  • Give a participation reward to everyone who joined, such as a certificate or a thank-you message from leadership.
  • If you want a longer goal, pool the team's climbing into a trek-sized target and plan the real trek together. Our easy treks guide lists good first options.

Mistakes to avoid

  1. No clear rules. People quit when they cannot tell how they are being scored.
  2. Totals instead of averages. Small teams give up in week one.
  3. Public individual leaderboards. They shame people who are starting out.
  4. Setting one big target. Ten thousand steps suits some bodies and schedules, and not others.
  5. Forgetting remote, shift and travelling staff. Use averages and let each person report their own days.
  6. Collecting more data than needed. It adds risk and no value.
  7. Stopping on the last day without a finish. A short wrap-up message or lunch is what turns a challenge into a habit.

A simple four-week plan

  • Week 0: announce, form teams, share the rules.
  • Weeks 1 to 4: post a short team update every Monday; highlight effort, not just rank.
  • Week 5: announce results, give prizes, ask what to change next time.

A sample rules page

Paste this into your announcement and edit it:

  • What: a four-week team step challenge, open to everyone, optional to join.
  • When: start and end dates, with the last day to join.
  • Teams: seven people each; teams are ranked by average daily steps per person.
  • Counting: any phone, watch or manual entry; minutes of other activity can be entered too.
  • Privacy: only steps or minutes are collected, shown as team boards, deleted after the challenge.
  • Prizes: team lunch for the top average, a small prize for the most improved, and thanks for everyone.
General information, not medical or legal advice. People with a health condition should speak to a doctor before joining any activity challenge.

Quick questions

How long should a workplace step challenge run?

Three to four weeks is a good start: long enough to build a habit, short enough to stay interesting. Run a short wrap-up the week after.

How many steps should be the target?

Do not set one number for everyone. Let people set a personal goal and rank teams by average steps, so no one is held to a target that does not suit them.

What if someone cheats by shaking their phone?

Keep stakes small (recognition over cash) and cap daily counts at a reasonable maximum you announce up front. If the rewards are friendly, there is little reason to cheat.

Is collecting step counts personal data?

Yes, step counts tied to a named person are personal data. Collect only what you need, tell people why and how it will be used, and let them withdraw. Ask your legal or HR team to check your plan.
