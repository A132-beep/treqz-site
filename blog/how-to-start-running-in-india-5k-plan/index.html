How to start running in India: a 5K plan for beginners

A run/walk 8-week plan to your first 5K, plus what to wear, where to run safely in Indian cities, and how to pace yourself. For complete beginners.

The 30-second answer
  • Run three days a week and mix running with walking. Each week, run a little more and walk a little less.
  • Go slowly enough to hold a conversation. Most beginners start too fast.
  • Run early or late in the day, in a park or a quiet lit road, and see a doctor first if you have a health condition.

You can go from no running to a 5K in about two months by mixing short runs with walking, three days a week, and adding a little each week. The method is the same one used by the NHS Couch to 5K programme, and the plan below adapts it for Indian conditions.

Why a run/walk plan works

New runners usually quit because the first weeks feel too hard. The fix is to run for short stretches and walk in between, so you finish each session feeling that you could do a little more. The NHS Couch to 5K is built this way: it takes nine weeks, uses three runs a week with rest days between, and starts with one-minute runs followed by a minute and a half of walking. By the end you run for 30 minutes without stopping. You can repeat any week if it felt hard; there is no deadline.

It also helps to know the bigger picture. The World Health Organization recommends that adults get at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity a week, and links regular activity to lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure, and to better sleep and mental health. Running three times a week gets you there.

If you have a heart, lung or joint condition, are pregnant, or are simply worried, talk to a doctor before you begin. The NHS gives the same advice.

The 8-week 5K plan

Do three sessions a week, for example Monday, Wednesday and Saturday. Start every session with a 5-minute brisk walk, and finish with a 5-minute easy walk. Jog slowly. If you cannot talk in short sentences, slow down or walk.

How to pace yourself

  • Use the talk test. You should be able to say a short sentence while running. If you are gasping, you are going too fast.
  • Ignore speed for now. The NHS programme says to go at a pace that feels right for you. Speed comes later.
  • Walk without guilt. Walking breaks are part of the plan, not a failure.
  • Keep rest days. Your body gets fitter on the days you rest. Do not run on consecutive days in the first month.

Mild muscle soreness is normal; sharp or growing pain in a joint, shin or foot is not. Skip a session and, if it stays, see a doctor or physiotherapist. See our guide to beginner running injuries.

Make it a habit

  • Fix the days and time. Put your three sessions in your calendar like meetings. Early morning, before the day gets busy, works for many people.
  • Lay out your kit the night before, so there is nothing to decide at 6 a.m.
  • Track your sessions in a notebook or an app. Seeing a row of ticks is motivating.
  • Find company. A friend, a colleague or a club makes it easier to show up. See how to join a run club in India.
  • Be patient. If you miss a week because of illness, travel or work, repeat the last week you finished and carry on. The NHS plan says you can repeat any week until you feel ready for the next.

What to wear and carry

  • Shoes: proper running shoes that fit well matter more than any other kit. See running shoes for Indian roads.
  • Clothes: light, breathable fabric. Cotton gets heavy with sweat. Avoid new clothes or shoes on the day of a long session.
  • Water: carry some, or plan a route past a water point, especially in summer. Our guide to running in Indian heat and monsoon covers the details.
  • Phone and ID: keep a phone and an emergency contact with you.

Where to run safely in India

  • Choose the place: parks, college grounds, tracks and wide, well-lit roads with a footpath. Avoid roads with fast, heavy traffic and no footpath.
  • Choose the time: early morning or evening, when it is cooler. Choose lit routes if you run in the dark, and wear something bright.
  • Be seen and aware: run facing oncoming traffic when there is no footpath, keep the volume low if you use earphones, and watch for uneven ground, stray dogs and open drains.
  • Tell someone: share your route and time, or run with a friend. Many cities have free weekend group runs; see how to join a run club and our run clubs page.

Every park and city has its own rules and opening hours, so check locally before you go.

What comes after 5K?

Once you can run 5K comfortably, you can add distance or just enjoy it. A natural next step is our 8-week plan to 10K. If you like running with others, Treqz Crew and Treqz Run (coming soon) are being built for teams and clubs, but the plan above needs nothing except a pair of shoes.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you feel chest pain, dizziness or unusual breathlessness, stop and get medical help.

Quick questions

Can I start running if I have never run?

Yes. Beginner programmes start with short runs of about a minute followed by walking. If you have a health condition or any worry, talk to a doctor before you start.

How long does it take to run 5K?

The NHS Couch to 5K programme takes nine weeks, and you can repeat any week until you feel ready. Our table below is eight weeks; take ten or twelve if you need to.

How many days a week should a beginner run?

Three. The NHS programme uses three runs a week with rest days in between, so your body can adapt.

Is it safe to run on Indian roads?

It can be, if you choose well. Prefer parks, tracks and wide lit roads, run facing traffic, and avoid headphones at full volume. See the safety section below.
